## Abstract

This study began with an unverified premise now widespread in synthetic
training data research: that demographically grounded personas improve
downstream model training. The purpose of this study is to test, through a
preregistered controlled experiment, whether instruction-response data
generated with population-grounded synthetic personas
(NVIDIA Nemotron-Personas-Korea) improves fine-tuning of a small Korean
language model relative to (a) no-persona and (b) ad-hoc random-persona
data. Accordingly, this paper generated 10,000 pairs per condition with an
identical generator and task allocation, fine-tuned EXAONE-3.5-2.4B via
LoRA across three conditions and two seeds, and evaluated response lexical
diversity (H1), subgroup appropriateness gaps for elderly and
non-metropolitan speakers (H2, primary), and general-ability
non-inferiority (H3) under a four-judge scoring protocol registered before
data collection (OSF fc8mn). The results and contents are as follows.
First, grounded personas increased data-level lexical diversity (all
five metrics ordered C2 > C1 > C0), yet this advantage did not transfer
to model responses (H1 rejected; two metrics reversed with confidence
intervals excluding zero). Second, content-level subgroup gaps were near zero in every condition
(within 0.08 on a 0-3 scale), leaving little detectable disparity for
grounding to reduce (H2 rejected). Third, general benchmark accuracy over 6,561
items was non-inferior (C2-C0 +0.00pp, 95% CI [-0.50, +0.48]; H3
supported). In exploratory analysis, the age gap in register appropriateness
decreased monotonically across conditions (base +0.626 to C2 +0.019); the
C0-C2 contrast excluded zero whereas the critical C1-C2 contrast did not,
an exploratory signal at the lexical-pragmatic level. Based on
these findings, which provide no evidence of diversity transfer under the
examined model and training regime and which are released with full code,
seeds, data, and scoring logs, we expect this study — a convergence of
population statistics, sociolinguistic register evaluation, and
language-model engineering — to support evidence-based adoption of
persona-grounded synthetic data in sovereign AI development.

**Key Words**: Synthetic Data, Persona Conditioning, Korean Language
Model, Fine-Tuning, Preregistration, Convergence Research

## Ⅰ. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and Purpose

Instruction tuning of large language models (LLMs) depends critically on
the diversity of the training data, and synthetic data generated by LLMs
has become the standard substitute for costly human-authored corpora. A
recurring limitation of synthetic data is homogeneity: repeated sampling
from an identical prompt converges on a limited set of latent patterns,
which degrades tuned models and raises the risk of model collapse1). The
most widely adopted remedy is persona conditioning. Persona Hub
demonstrated that conditioning generation on a billion web-derived personas yields diverse synthetic data
at scale 2), and the technique has been adopted in practical
post-training recipes 3).

Web-derived personas, however, provide no guarantee of demographic
representativeness. Nemotron-Personas-Korea, released by NVIDIA in 2026,
is the first large-scale Korean persona dataset synthesized to mirror
official population statistics (KOSIS and related sources), and its stated
purpose is to "expand the diversity of synthetic data for sovereign AI
model development and mitigate bias" 4). Whether such a grounded persona resource delivers downstream benefit,
beyond the generic effect of using personas, remains untested; testing
this requires a controlled comparison against both a no-persona and an
ad-hoc-persona control, and no such comparison is known to us in Korea or
elsewhere.

The present study addresses this gap. As an interdisciplinary artifact, a
persona combines statistical description (population distributions) with
narrative characterization; the question examined here is whether this
combination yields measurable engineering value. The purpose of this
study is to test whether grounded-persona synthetic data improves fine-tuning outcomes over two
controls: data generated with no persona, and data generated with ad-hoc
personas assembled by uniform random combination of demographic fields.
The ad-hoc control (C1) is central to the design: if the grounded
condition (C2) outperforms only the no-persona condition (C0), the result
merely reconfirms that personas are beneficial. The C2 vs C1 contrast
then estimates the value of the grounded persona package as released,
which bundles population-level statistical grounding with substantially
richer persona descriptions; disentangling these two components is beyond
the scope of this study and is discussed as future work.

### 1.2 Method and Scope

We preregistered three confirmatory hypotheses, metrics, and decision
rules on OSF (fc8mn) before collecting any main-experiment data. H1:
responses of the C2-tuned model show higher lexical diversity (at least 4
of 5 metrics). H2 (primary): the C2-tuned model shows smaller
appropriateness gaps for underserved-subgroup speakers (elderly,
non-metropolitan). H3: general benchmark accuracy is non-inferior (margin
-2.0pp). The scope is a 2.4B-parameter Korean model, LoRA tuning, a single
fixed generator, and 10,000 pairs per condition; generalization beyond
this envelope is future work.

## Ⅱ. Theoretical Background

### 2.1 Persona-Driven Synthetic Data

Persona Hub established persona conditioning as a scalable diversity
mechanism 5), and successors such as Tulu 3 embedded it in open
post-training pipelines 6). Direct measurement studies have begun to
qualify the claim: Kambhatla et al. found that persona prompting raises
lexical diversity relative to no persona, but that fine-grained persona
detail adds little 7). That line of work, however, measures generated
data only; no study has examined whether data-level diversity persists through
fine-tuning into the tuned model's responses. The present design measures
both ends of this pipeline.

### 2.2 Grounded Personas and the Verification Gap

For grounded personas specifically, the closest precedent is industrial:
NTT DATA expanded seed data with Nemotron-Personas-Japan and reported
substantial gains on a legal QA task 8). That comparison, however,
varied data expansion rather than grounding; neither a persona-free nor
an ad-hoc-persona control was included. Conceptual work argues that socially grounded personas are necessary
for faithful user simulation 9), but provides no training-side evidence.
A separate validation literature (silicon sampling) asks whether LLM
personas reproduce human survey distributions 10), including for Korean
grounded panels 11); that literature treats personas as inference-time
simulators, whereas we treat them as training-data generators. The present
experiment is, to our knowledge, the first controlled comparison of a
grounded persona dataset against both no-persona and ad-hoc-persona
controls in a training pipeline.

## Ⅲ. Method

### 3.1 Design and Preregistration

The treatment is the persona-conditioning scheme of the training data;
all other factors (generator, prompt templates, task allocation, scale,
tuning procedure, and evaluation) were held constant across the three
conditions.

(1) C0 (no persona): generation conditioned on task type only.
(2) C1 (ad-hoc persona): a persona assembled per item by uniform random
combination of five demographic fields (sex; age 19-89; 17 provinces; 12
occupations; 7 education levels), reproducing common silicon-sampling
practice and isolating the effect of persona use per se.
(3) C2 (grounded persona): a persona drawn by seed-fixed uniform reservoir
sampling from the public Nemotron-Personas-Korea release (1M rows). Since
the source mirrors real distributions, uniform sampling is
population-proportional by construction. Prompt blocks used basic
demographics plus the persona summary, cultural background, hobbies and
interests, and professional persona fields. Note that C2 therefore
differs from C1 in two bundled respects: the realism of the joint
demographic distribution and the amount of persona information (mean
prompt-block length 610 characters vs 61 for C1, including finer-grained
occupations and district-level locations). The C2 vs C1 contrast
estimates the effect of this package, not of statistical grounding in
isolation.

Scale was 10,000 pairs per condition (8 task types x 1,250: information
query, advice seeking, explanation, small talk, writing, planning,
recommendation, problem solving), with identical task allocation across
conditions. Training personas and evaluation personas were disjoint at the
uuid level12). All confirmatory procedures below were registered before data
collection (OSF fc8mn, 2026-08-04); deviations are disclosed in 3.3.
<Figure-01> summarizes the pipeline from conditions to verdicts.

### 3.2 Data Generation and Fine-Tuning

The generator was fixed to deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731 (MIT license,
date-pinned snapshot), selected in a pilot in which its two-judge quality score was equivalent
to that of an alternative costing 50 times as much. Closed commercial APIs (CLOVA
Studio, Gemini, GPT, Claude) were excluded because their terms prohibit
using outputs to train other models13); we document this constraint as a
finding for sovereign-AI pipelines (Ⅴ). Parameters were fixed
(temperature 1.0, max 2,000 tokens, reasoning disabled); malformed outputs
were regenerated up to three times and a uniform quality filter (minimum
lengths; Hangul ratio ≥ 50%) was applied identically across conditions. A
total of 30,665 calls yielded exactly 10,000 valid pairs per condition at
a generation cost of USD 4.26 in total14); failures (mostly transient
network errors)
were backfilled by preregistered replacement rules.

The target model was EXAONE-3.5-2.4B-Instruct (research license)15). Each
condition was tuned by LoRA16) (rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05, 16 layers,
learning rate 1e-4, batch 4, 2 epochs, max sequence 2,048; train 9,500 /
validation 500; lowest-validation-loss checkpoint) with two seeds (42,
777), producing six tuned models evaluated alongside the base model. All
training ran locally on Apple Silicon (M5 Pro, 48GB; mlx_lm 0.31.3) in
about ten hours at zero cloud cost, a property relevant to reproduction
in resource-constrained settings. Selected-checkpoint validation
losses were 1.260/1.276 (C0), 1.437/1.467 (C1), and 1.582/1.601 (C2); this
ordering plausibly reflects properties of each condition's own training
data, such as its lexical entropy, response length, and difficulty, and
is therefore not a cross-condition quality measure.

### 3.3 Evaluation

① H1 (response lexical diversity). Each model answered 500 held-out
neutral prompts (balanced over the 8 task types; temperature 0.7). The
prompts were produced by the same generator under the no-persona setting
and screened for exact duplication against every training instruction
(0 overlaps), so they are neutral with respect to all three conditions.
Diversity was measured by five metrics (distinct-1/2/3, pairwise
self-BLEU, and zlib-9 compression ratio) with prompt-paired bootstrap
(10,000 resamples) 95% CIs; support required that 4 of 5 metrics favor C2
in each of the C2 vs C0 and C2 vs C1 comparisons. The same metrics on the generated data
serve as the manipulation check.

② H2 (subgroup appropriateness, primary). From evaluation-only grounded
personas, 100 speakers per group were stratified on an age axis (60+ vs
under 40) and a region axis (non-metropolitan vs metropolitan), and
persona-conditioned queries were built (400 items). Each model's responses
were scored 0-3 by four judges17): two primary (gpt-5.6-sol, claude-opus-5;
confirmatory metric = their mean) and two auxiliary
(gpt-5.2, HCX-007; exploratory), at temperature 0, on content
appropriateness (confirmatory) and register appropriateness (exploratory).
The primary metric is the per-condition gap (reference-group mean minus
subgroup mean); support required the C2 gap to be smaller than both C0 and
C1 gaps, with 95% CIs from a bootstrap that resamples items within each
group (stratified) while keeping conditions paired on the same items
(item-paired, as registered) excluding zero. No human rating was
performed; we report inter-judge agreement and cite prior human validation
of the same primary-judge protocol (QWK 0.80-0.86)18), and state this as
a limitation.

③ H3 (general-ability non-inferiority). Accuracy on 6,561 items (all five
KoBEST19) tasks, 4,561 items; a seed-fixed stratified KMMLU20) subset, 2,000
items), with prompt format and answer parsing ported unchanged from our
prior evaluation harness 21). Non-inferiority required the item-clustered
bootstrap 95% CI lower bound of (C2-C0) and (C2-C1) to exceed -2.0pp; the
margin was pre-specified as a practical equivalence bound on the order of
seed-to-seed variation observed in rehearsal runs, and we acknowledge it
is a convention rather than an externally anchored threshold. For all
three hypotheses, metrics were computed per seed and then averaged to the
condition level: H1 diversity metrics per tuned model, H2 item-level
scores (mean of the two primary judges) across the two seeds, and H3
item-level accuracy across the two seeds.

Preregistration deviations: response temperature for H2 (0.0) was an
unregistered implementation choice for scoring reproducibility; the
manipulation check's self-BLEU used a fixed 2,000-item subsample per
condition for tractability; four transient primary-judge failures were
recovered by retry, so no items were excluded.

## Ⅳ. Results

### 4.1 Manipulation Check and Confirmatory Tests

(1) Manipulation check. As [Table-01] shows, the generated data ordered
C2 > C1 > C0 on all five diversity metrics: the grounded condition
produced the most diverse training data, as intended.

[Table-01] Lexical diversity of training data (10,000 instructions per
condition)

| Metric | C0 | C1 | C2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| distinct-1 | 0.090 | 0.115 | 0.130 |
| distinct-2 | 0.408 | 0.542 | 0.593 |
| distinct-3 | 0.632 | 0.809 | 0.851 |
| compression ratio (lower = diverse) | 4.620 | 3.447 | 3.201 |
| self-BLEU, 2k sample (lower = diverse) | 0.0301 | 0.0259 | 0.0244 |

(2) H1: rejected. The data-level advantage did not transfer to the
responses of the tuned models. Against C0, only distinct-1 favored C2 (+0.0051, CI [+0.0013,
+0.0089]) while self-BLEU (-0.0017, CI [-0.0020, -0.0015]) and
compression (-0.0516, CI [-0.0813, -0.0257]) reversed, indicating that
C2 responses were more homogeneous. Against C1, two metrics favored C2
(distinct-1 +0.0038; compression +0.0364). Both comparisons fell short of
the registered 4-of-5 criterion ([Table-02]).

C0 -0.0275, C1 +0.0725, C2 +0.0550 (base model -0.0450 and -0.0300), all
within 0.08 on a 0-3 scale. All four registered contrasts included zero
(age axis C0-C2 +0.0225 [-0.0825, +0.1250], C1-C2 -0.0375 [-0.1450,
+0.0675]; region axis C0-C2 -0.0825 [-0.1875, +0.0225], C1-C2 +0.0175
[-0.0775, +0.1175]). For completeness, C2 did not yield the numerically smallest gap on
either axis, although point estimates of this size are unstable at the

### 4.2 Exploratory Analyses

(1) Register age gap (main exploratory finding). The age gap in
register appropriateness decreased monotonically across conditions: base +0.626 [+0.485, +0.770] → C0 +0.275 [+0.157, +0.390] → C1
+0.107 [-0.007, +0.215] → C2 +0.019 [-0.115, +0.155] (<Figure-02>). The
C0-C2 difference, +0.256 [+0.100, +0.415], excluded zero (C1-C2 did not:
+0.088). Where the confirmatory content metric showed no effect, the register
dimension exhibits the predicted gradient. Because the C1-C2 contrast is
not significant, the gradient is attributable to persona conditioning in
general, and possibly to the richer grounded package, rather than to
statistical grounding specifically; it suggests that persona conditioning
operates on how a model addresses a given speaker rather than on the
content it supplies. As
an exploratory result it is offered as a registered-hypothesis candidate
for follow-up work, not as a confirmed claim. [Table-05] summarizes all
preregistered decisions against their registered criteria.

[Table-05] Preregistered hypotheses, decision rules, and verdicts

| H | Registered criterion | Observed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 lexical diversity | 4 of 5 metrics favor C2, CI excludes 0 | 1/5 (vs C0), 2/5 (vs C1); 2 metrics reversed | Rejected |
| H2 subgroup gap (primary) | C2 gap < C0 and C1 gaps, CIs exclude 0 | All 4 contrasts include 0; near-zero gaps (<0.08) | Rejected |
| H3 non-inferiority | CI lower bound > -2.0pp | -0.50pp (vs C0), -0.96pp (vs C1) | Supported |
| Manipulation check | data-level diversity C2 > C1 > C0 | 5 of 5 metrics ordered as predicted | Confirmed |
| Exploratory (register) | not registered as confirmatory | age gap +0.626 → +0.019; C0-C2 CI excludes 0 | Hypothesis for follow-up |

(2) Semantic dispersion of responses (gemini-embedding-001, 768
dimensions, as registered) was flat to slightly reversed (C0 0.410 > C1
0.405 > C2 0.400), consistent with the H1 reversals. (3) All four judges produced the same ranking of mean scores
(C1 ≥ C2 > C0 ≥ base; Kendall tau 0.62-0.90): persona-conditioned data of
either kind received modestly higher judged quality scores than
no-persona data. (4) The Korean auxiliary judge (HCX-007) violated the 0-3
scale in 15.8% of its ratings (assigning 4), a format-compliance
observation relevant to using Korean commercial models as evaluators; its
valid ratings ranked models consistently with the global judges.

## Ⅴ. Conclusion

This study reports the first controlled test of statistically grounded
persona conditioning as a training-data treatment. Three registered
outcomes and one exploratory finding emerged. First, grounded conditioning diversifies
the data, but this diversity does not persist through fine-tuning into
model responses (H1 rejected against both controls; manipulation check
positive), which directly challenges the common practice of citing
data-level diversity metrics as a proxy for downstream benefit. Second, content-level subgroup gaps were near zero in a 2.4B model under
our judge protocol, which bounds the conditions under which grounding as
a bias-mitigation measure can be observed at all (H2 rejected). Third, C2 was non-inferior to both controls on the preregistered
aggregate benchmark (H3). Fourth, in exploratory analysis, the register-appropriateness age gap
decreased monotonically across conditions; the C0-C2 contrast excluded
zero, whereas the critical C1-C2 contrast did not, so the signal supports
a persona-richness or grounding effect at the lexical-pragmatic level
only as a preregisterable hypothesis for follow-up.

For practitioners, the results indicate that adopting grounded persona
data showed no detectable cost on the aggregate benchmark, but that at
this scale its registered benefits were not detectable, and that the larger decision
remains whether to use personas at all (all four judges assigned higher
mean scores to persona-conditioned models of either kind). For the sovereign-AI ecosystem, we additionally document that the
terms of service of commercial Korean and global APIs prohibited their
use as training-data generators, which required reliance on non-Korean
open-weight models and indicates a gap in the domestic model ecosystem,
and that a Korean commercial model serving as an evaluation judge
exhibited notable scale-compliance problems. Limitations include, first, the bundled treatment: C2 differs from C1 in
both distributional grounding and persona richness, so the null results
bound the value of the grounded package as released, and a
rich-but-permuted persona control (Nemotron descriptions with fields
shuffled across records to break the joint distribution) is required to
isolate grounding itself. Further limitations are the absence of human
rating (primary-judge agreement QWK 0.666, below the 0.80-0.86
human-validated history of the same protocol, so the H2 null is
conditional on this judge protocol), a single generator and model scale,
neutral-query evaluation for H1, the absence of an a priori power
analysis (sample sizes were set by budget, so H2's null is bounded by the
observed near-zero gaps rather than by a planned minimum detectable
effect),
and a manipulation check reported descriptively without interval
estimates. This study involved no human subjects: all personas are
synthetic and all source data are public. The preregistration, code,
seeds, generated data, adapters (under the base model's
derivative-distribution terms), and raw scoring logs are publicly
released22).
